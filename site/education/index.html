What this course is not

An AI literacy course.   A prompt engineering workshop.   An intro to programming.

The Thai government, the universities and the big vendors all teach those for free right now. We can't compete with free — and we won't charge you for something you can get for nothing.

What nobody else is doing is the thing below.

Why high school, not university

01

University students carry a debt that high schoolers don't.

They were taught syntax → data structures → frameworks → then you may build something. That sunk cost makes them reluctant to skip ahead, and a declared major kills ideas early — "that's not my field." A 16-year-old never learned that route, so when AI hands them the ability to build, they don't feel like they're cheating.

02

They don't know where the edges are, so they don't stay inside them.

This is the most valuable thing in the room — and the easiest for us to destroy. Teach engineering discipline too early and the imagination goes out with it. So we don't. Style, architecture and naming get taught the day a student is genuinely annoyed by their own mess, and not one week before.

03

The ceiling was never technical.

It sits in three places: not knowing what is even possible to make, never having carried one idea all the way to a finished thing, and making things nobody ever uses. So the course does exactly three jobs — protect the imagination, supply the ability to build, and manufacture real feedback.

Twelve weeks, four steps

Week 1–2

Break the shell

Something worth showing a classmate, made in the first 90 minutes. We hand them a working card and get out of the way — no theory in week one.

They leave with: a thing they can send to a friend
Week 3–5

Fix my own annoyance

Start from "what do you redo every single day and hate?" — the same question we ask a company's staff on a Discovery Sprint. Then build the thing that kills it.

They leave with: a small tool they personally use
Week 6–9

Make something someone else will use

Find three real users outside the school and iterate until those people are actually using it. Classmates cheering for each other doesn't count.

They leave with: users. Actual ones.
Week 10–12

Publish, then stand up and explain it

Ship to a public URL, then present at a Demo Day with parents and school leadership in the room.

They leave with: a public link for their university portfolio

Weeks 6–9 are where the ceiling actually breaks. The moment a stranger uses something you made is the moment it stops being homework. Everything before it still is.

University courses are usually missing exactly this stretch — assignments get graded and then nobody ever opens them again. A student can finish four years of projects without a single piece of real user feedback.

And if a student can't find three users, that is the most valuable result of the term. It means the idea solved nobody's problem. We say so, they change direction, and they've learned something most adults haven't.

What a week looks like

SegmentTimeWhat happens
Show & tell15 min Students present, not the teacher. Whoever got a real user this week goes first.
This week's step20 min Only what's needed for this week. Nothing extra.
Build60 min Teacher and assistant in the room. They do not write it for them — help arrives only after a student has been stuck for ten minutes.
Commit10 min Each student says out loud what they'll have by next week, and writes it down.

The show & tell isn't optional. No lecture we give competes with one classmate saying "my mum actually uses this."

One condition, and it is not negotiable

At least one teacher takes the whole course alongside the students. Not an administrator whose name is on a form.

  • If nobody at the school owns this, the course dies the day we leave — and what you're left with is a pile of unused accounts.
  • That teacher joins a two-day workshop before week one. They don't need to know how to code. They need to know how to not write it for the student, how to judge an idea by "would anyone use this" rather than "is this technically hard," and what to do with a student who is stuck.
  • The school must allow student work to be published on the public internet. Ask this early — discovering it's forbidden in week 10 kills the whole term.

What we deliver, per class

  1. Two-day teacher workshop, plus a teacher handbook
  2. Twelve weekly lesson plans — each stating what to teach, what to build, what to check
  3. Experience-card credits for the whole class, with a usage dashboard
  4. Weekly archive of student work as public links
  5. One online clinic at week 6 — the pivot week
  6. On-site support for Demo Day
  7. A class portfolio page — one public link per student, usable in university applications
  8. End-of-term report: completion, which projects found real users, and what the teacher recommends changing next time

Item 7 is what the school will actually show the outside world — and the only part parents will understand without explanation.

How you know it worked

  • All eight deliverables handed over
  • At least 60% of the class has a working public URL
  • At least 3 students have real users from outside the school
  • Demo Day happened, with school leadership and parents present

Why 60% and not everyone: promising 100% has exactly two outcomes — we quietly build it for them, or we lie at handover. Both are worse than 60%. These two numbers are our starting assumptions and we'll revise them against the first real class rather than defend them.

When you should not buy this

  • No teacher will commit to the full term. We won't sell it. See the condition above.
  • You want a one-off AI awareness talk. Go take a free one — the government and the universities are running them. Charging you for the same thing would be theft.
  • You want us to build the student projects for a competition or an inspection. We decline this one without discussing price. Doing it once makes every claim on this page a lie.
  • You want it delivered to 600 students at once. One class at a time. Scale is what makes this kind of course meaningless.
  • Student work can't be published publicly. Then the mechanism that makes the course work doesn't exist. Change the rule, or don't buy the course.

Start with a free call. We'll ask which teacher would own it and whether student work can go public — if the answers aren't there yet, we'll tell you to wait.
